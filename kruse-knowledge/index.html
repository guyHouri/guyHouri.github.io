LDL receptor mechanismRead full source →
Jack corrects a widespread misreading of Brown and Goldstein's Nobel Prize work. Their research focused on the classic LDL receptor (LDLR), not the scavenger receptor. Native, undamaged LDL is actively recognized by LDLR on liver and peripheral cell surfaces, allowing cells to internalize it for membrane synthesis and hormone production. Healthy cells tightly regulate this process via a feedback loop: when a cell has enough cholesterol, it suppresses its own LDL receptors to prevent overloading. The disease driver is not native LDL quantity but oxidized LDL. When LDL is oxidized by inflammation, seed oils, high blood sugar, or cigarette smoke, the scavenger receptor unlocks. Macrophages recognize oxidized LDL as a threat, engulf it, swell into foam cells, and build arterial plaque. The Nobel Prize answered the question of what damages LDL - oxidation - but medicine ignored the answer and continued lowering total LDL instead of addressing oxidation drivers.
LDL receptor (LDLR): A protein on cell surfaces that recognizes and binds native LDL particles, allowing cells to take them up and use their cholesterol content. LDLR has built-in feedback regulation that prevents excessive cholesterol uptake.
feedback loop: A regulatory mechanism where cells suppress LDL receptor expression when they have sufficient cholesterol, preventing excessive cholesterol accumulation. This is the key finding of Brown and Goldstein's work.
oxidized LDL: LDL particles that have been chemically modified by oxidation, typically caused by inflammation, high blood sugar, seed oils, or cigarette smoke. The immune system recognizes oxidized LDL as foreign and dangerous.
scavenger receptor: A cell surface receptor that recognizes and binds oxidized LDL and other modified lipoproteins. Unlike LDLR, it lacks feedback regulation, allowing unlimited uptake. This uncontrolled uptake leads to foam cell formation.
foam cells: Macrophages that have engulfed oxidized LDL and become engorged with lipid droplets. They accumulate in arterial walls and form the core of atherosclerotic plaques. Foam cell formation is the first visible step in arterial disease.
