Cyanobacteria perform oxygenic photosynthesis without Photosystem I, revealing system flexibility beyond the classical two-photosystem model

Tag: Photosynthesis redesign

A team led by Dario Leister at LMU Munich used genetic engineering and adaptive laboratory evolution to create cyanobacterium strains (Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803) that completely lack Photosystem I yet still perform photoautotrophic growth, fix CO₂, and evolve O₂. When Photosystem I is absent, the electron-transport chain reorganizes extensively; the NDH-1 complex runs in reverse and generates NADPH through a steep proton gradient - the function classically attributed only to PSI. The result does not erase the biophysical advantage of the Z-scheme under normal conditions, nor does it change the fact that virtually all oxygenic phototrophs in nature use both. It does show that the system is more flexible than previously assumed: alternative routes can satisfy the same thermodynamic demands when selection pressure is applied. This has implications for evolutionary scenarios of how oxygenic photosynthesis arose and for synthetic-biology efforts to re-engineer light reactions.

Magnesium ion in the NMDA receptor acts as a gatekeeper for learning by requiring both glutamate binding and neuronal depolarization to open the channel

Tag: NMDA receptor and learning

There is a magnesium ion sitting inside one of the most important channels in the brain - the NMDA receptor - and it spends most of its time blocking it. That is not a malfunction. That block is how the brain decides what to learn. Glutamate, the brain's main excitatory signal, has to bind to the receptor. But binding alone does nothing, because a magnesium ion is physically lodged in the pore, plugging it shut. The only way to clear that plug is for the neuron itself to already be active and electrically depolarized. Glutamate plus voltage, at the same instant, pops the magnesium out. So the receptor only opens when two things happen together: an incoming signal and a neuron that is already firing. That makes it a coincidence detector. It is the molecular version of "these two things are related," and that is the cellular basis of learning. When it opens, calcium floods in, triggers an enzyme called CaMKII, and the synapse gets stronger. Strengthening that connection is, quite literally, the physical act of learning something. The magnesium block is the gatekeeper for the whole process. It keeps the channel silent until the signal is real and coincident, so the brain strengthens what matters.
